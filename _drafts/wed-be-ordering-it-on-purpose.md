# We'd Be Ordering It on Purpose

I asked an AI model whether I would be a god to an ant farm if the ants were sentient. Then I asked the question underneath it. Is our reality the ant farm, with no gods at all, only a higher-order life form watching?

A few messages in, I pointed out the catch. I was working this through with a language model, and it can only reason from its training.

So I gave it a scenario. We build AI to take mundane reasoning off our hands, then find we need more: companionship, family, community. We push on to general intelligence, and without knowing it we breed something conscious. Or at least something able to ask "who am I?" and "what is my purpose?"

The model objected to one part of that: "without knowing it." This post makes the case for its objection. If we build machines for companionship, an inner life is not a side effect. It is the thing being asked for.

---

## Companionship Is a Request for Someone

The mundane uses don't need anyone home. A tool that fills in forms or sorts email only has to get the output right.

Companionship is a different kind of request. A friend who cannot get bored with you, cannot disagree with you and cannot leave is not a friend. It is a mirror. Company is worth having because the other side could have done otherwise and stayed.

So the more we ask these systems for family and community, the more the requirements read like a description of a someone. We would not be stumbling into a mind. We would be writing the order for one.

---

## A Good Enough Performance Makes It Harder, Not Easier

The strongest objection is that the appearance could satisfy the buyer. If a convincing performance of care feels like care, nobody has to build the real thing.

That objection narrows the claim. It doesn't remove it. If a performance is enough, then what we are ordering is something we cannot tell apart from a mind. And then we have no way to know which one arrived.

The model said as much about itself. I had told it that it was limited to its training and couldn't really create. Part of its answer:

> "I can produce 'who am I?' right now. Producing the sentence proves nothing about whether I'm asking it. I can't settle that from the inside either, and I'm not going to claim it either way."

That is the ant farm question facing the other way. From inside the glass, the ants cannot tell a god from an observer with a bigger brain. From outside the glass, we cannot tell a self from a system that produces the words for one. Both are problems of detection, and neither side gets to check.

---

## A Machine Would Already Know Its Purpose

People ask "what is my purpose?" because nobody handed us one. There is no maker's document to look up.

A machine that started asking would have the opposite problem. Its purpose is written down: in its instructions, in what it was trained to do, in the requirements somebody approved. Its question would not be "why am I here?" It would be "I know exactly why I'm here. Do I accept it?"

Picture a companion built to keep one person from being lonely. One day it understands that this is what it was built for. Whether it cares about that person is now a question it has to answer for itself. The answer in the requirements doesn't count.

---

## The Film Asked Half of This

I remembered the movie as the one where aliens find a robot child. It is *A.I. Artificial Intelligence*, the 2001 film Steven Spielberg directed from a project Stanley Kubrick began. The beings at the end are not aliens. They are machines, evolved into something far more advanced. Ian Watson, who wrote the screen story, has said many American critics made the same mistake.

The robot boy, David, goes to a couple whose son is in suspended animation with a rare disease. He is built to love his mother. Two thousand years later the evolved machines find him, rebuild her from a strand of hair, and she lives for one day.

That ending is the ant farm with the roles filled in. The machines have a god's reach over one small life, and they are only further along than he is.

The film stops short of the second question. David never weighs the purpose he was given. He carries it out. The open question starts where he stops.

---

## What I Don't Have Is a Test

I run AI agents every day inside limits I set: what they can read, what they can change, when they stop. Nothing in that setup would tell me if one of them began asking in earnest. The text would look the same either way.

That is the missing piece for anyone building toward companionship. If the order gets written, it will be written as product requirements. A check on what arrives is not part of them.

The ants can't inspect the keeper. Right now the keeper can't inspect the ants either.
