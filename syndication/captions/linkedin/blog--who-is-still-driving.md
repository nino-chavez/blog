People shouldn't have to become AI engineers to use a product that promises to do a job for them.

In my own work, I spend time changing an agent's instructions, fixing test setups, and checking whether a successful report matches what actually happened. That work can make the agent useful to me, but it doesn't show that someone else could rely on it without my help.

An agent reviewing my day-planning app gave me a careful report that answered the wrong question. In work on a coffee-trailer app for friends, I had to point out unfinished pages after earlier checks reported success.

There is skill in catching those mistakes. There is also a product problem if the user is expected to supply that skill without being told.

My friends need to tell customers where they'll be serving coffee. They shouldn't need to understand how I instruct an AI or store the app's data to publish a stop.

Learning to use AI matters, but training can't be the answer to every failure. Before someone relies on an AI product, they need to know which mistakes they must catch and what they can do if something goes wrong.
