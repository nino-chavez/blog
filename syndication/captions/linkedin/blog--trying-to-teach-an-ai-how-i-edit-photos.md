I wanted to build something that learned how I edit photos and gave me a better starting point in Lightroom. I already use presets, so applying settings automatically wasn't enough to make it useful.

We got the mechanics working: recover my saved edits, propose new settings, apply them to isolated copies, and compare the results without changing the originals.

Then we ran blind, side-by-side tests. I chose between two versions of each photograph without knowing which method produced either one. One used settings predicted for that photo. The other used a fixed recipe derived from my training edits.

On ten photos, I preferred the fixed recipe six times and called four ties. A simpler learned model lost seven and tied three on the same photos. I hadn't picked the learned edit in either round.

These were small, overlapping tests. They don't settle whether personalized editing can work. They also didn't show that our approach would save time against my normal preset-and-adjust workflow.

During the work, I asked whether this was offering anything better than a batch preset. Then I asked what the session had cost.

The main coding conversation alone came to about $795 at standard API rates. That was an estimate, not a verified charge, and it excluded workers and the photo models' API calls. The proposed $100 research budget hadn't covered the conversation supervising the work.

I've written up the experiment and the changes we're proposing: count the whole task, limit attempts, and say what a result would change before running another test. Those controls still need testing.

Asking for the retrospective was part of my involvement, too. Reviewing outputs wasn't enough; I had to question whether the experiment was worth continuing.
