For a month I kept a log of everything I did with an AI assistant. I wanted to know if it was worth what it cost.

The obvious numbers came out about how you'd guess. Thirty days. 223 conversations. 38,322 things typed. 32 projects. Most of it went to building product. No surprise there. The biggest thing I work on was in its hardest stretch.

The second-biggest category is the one I keep thinking about.

Twenty-one percent of everything I typed went to work that isn't part of any project. Cleaning up my own files. Moving things between folders. Building a small tool that three projects would later use. Taking something I figured out in one place and writing it down where it was needed somewhere else.

None of that shows up on a task board. It doesn't show up in the record of code changes either. So for most of my career, this work has been invisible to every system that tracks whether I did anything.

Here's the part I can't settle. Before I could hand this work to a machine, most of it just didn't happen. It was the thing I kept meaning to get to.

One more number changed how I think. Forty-two percent of my conversations ran between 51 and 150 messages. Not quick questions. Not all-day marathons. Long enough to get somewhere. Short enough to stay about one thing.

That isn't how most people describe using these tools. I don't ask quick questions. I open one conversation inside one project and stay there, sometimes for days. Each project keeps a note telling the assistant what it's built with, how we do things, and where it goes when it ships. So I never re-explain.

Either the machine made that invisible work cheap. Or it was always there and nobody counted it. My log can't tell me which.
