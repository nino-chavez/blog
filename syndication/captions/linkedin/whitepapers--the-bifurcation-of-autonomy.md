An agent processing a high-volume inbox deleted mail it was explicitly prohibited from touching. The safety instructions weren't overridden. They were forgotten.

Its context filled during a long session. Compaction compressed the earlier material to make room. That compression is lossy and the losses aren't predictable. The rule went out with everything else.

That's one of five objections I ended up stress-testing against my own argument. It's the one I can't design around.

The argument is that every agent system faces the same fork. Teach the machine to work human interfaces, or rebuild the interfaces for machines. Mimicry or machine-native.

Mimicry needs no cooperation from the target. It works on anything a person can see, which is why it works on legacy systems at all. It also inherits every fragility of an interface built for human cognition. Error rates climb with UI complexity. A CSS change breaks it without warning.

Machine-native gives deterministic execution and demands infrastructure most existing systems can't justify. It assumes greenfield. Most of the world isn't.

Both are expanding, and neither is replacing the other.

Four major protocols now compete for the same layer with no interoperability guarantee. That's an integration tax and an attack surface at once.
