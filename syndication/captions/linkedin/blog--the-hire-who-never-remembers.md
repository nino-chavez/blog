Over two weeks this fall, I typed "merge it" to my coding agents 42 times.

Counting every version of it, 201 of my 1,422 messages had one job: getting an agent to push, open a pull request, or merge work it had already finished.

Every session starts by reading a file of standing instructions. One line in mine said "Push after commit when asked." So every session finished the work, then waited for me to ask. Each "merge it" fixed one session. The next one read the same line and waited again.

That's my answer to a question I keep hearing: what kind of person is good at AI?

My bet is a skill, not a temperament. It's the written half of management: writing down what a newcomer needs, saying what done looks like, and checking for what the writing misses. The other half, coaching people and earning their trust, has nothing to carry it from one session to the next.

On October 9, I replaced the line. The cleanest comparison is pushes. Nudges asking for a push went from about thirty in two weeks to none, apart from two that were really new instructions. At my old share of messages, there would have been about five. It's one person's sessions, not a study.

Writing it down isn't the whole skill, though. In an earlier count, 9 of 17 corrections I made were about a rule already written into those instructions. The agent had the rule in front of it and did the other thing anyway. So I've started adding checks that refuse the wrong action outright. I haven't measured those yet.

The practical version: when an agent keeps making the same mistake, change what it reads instead of correcting it again in conversation. When it ignores what it reads, add a check that stops it.
