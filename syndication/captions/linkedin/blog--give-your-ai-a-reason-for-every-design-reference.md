The research started with Mobbin, a library of product screenshots and flows. We asked what else was worth considering, then compared the alternatives to those options. The goal was to decide what, if anything, to add to an existing AI design setup.

One useful finding came while choosing references: the closer match for a sports-tournament board came from a task-management app.

In a recent study, an initial scheduling search returned a calendar and a booking screen. The organizer's problem was more specific: compare the activity, next match, and work assignments across several courts during play.

A later search for dense tables with status and responsibility returned a closer structural match. It offered an arrangement to examine, not proof of a better final design.

That's the part to explain when giving an AI a reference. Name what should transfer: the way related facts line up, for example. Also name what should stay behind: the source product's sidebar, vocabulary, and unrelated controls.

Before asking for the next revision, put this beside the screenshot:

“Use this example to help the person decide ___. Borrow ___. Preserve ___. We'll check whether it helped by ___.”

The study didn't demonstrate that added references improved the generated app. The practical takeaway is a more specific assignment for the next attempt: give each reference a job you can inspect.
