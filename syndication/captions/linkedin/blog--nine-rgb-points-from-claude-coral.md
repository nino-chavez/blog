A colleague looked at my freshly redesigned site and typed one line into Slack: "feels very Claudeish, which may be completely intentional."

It wasn't intentional. And measurement made it worse: the site's copper accent sat nine points of red, three of green, and nine of blue from Claude's own brand coral, on a matching cream ground. My accent was, within a glance's error, another company's brand color.

My reply at the time was "it's so hard to prompt for taste." That was the wrong diagnosis.

Swapping the palette — for colors harvested from my own photography — changed almost nothing. The deeper tell was structural: every page ran the same formula. Mono eyebrow, giant headline, right-rail paragraph, card grid. The consistency of the formula was the tell.

And the source of that formula wasn't the model freelancing. Five days before the feedback arrived, a different model had already traced the sameness to inputs I own: my design docs authorized the mono labels and the giant display type, and my own site-generation archetype defaulted to dark mode and loud typography. Its verdict, verbatim: "The agentic tooling structured the exercise; it did not supply the taste."

The fix that worked was a budget, not a bigger style guide. The clean reference site I was chasing runs 402 lines of CSS and 13 design tokens with no style guide at all; mine ran 10,615 lines and 42 tokens. The system that shipped caps markers instead of documenting them: one accent per ground, zero or one photographs per page, at most one dark band — and it must be a photograph.
