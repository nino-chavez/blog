Imagine your navigation app quietly adding a detour because businesses along the route paid to be seen.

You obey the signs and arrive safely. You're a competent driver, but the route serves someone else's interests. More driving lessons wouldn't tell you who paid for it.

AI gives people new capabilities. Deciding who can be trusted to use them is a familiar problem: for work that affects others, authority should follow demonstrated competence. Shared rules need to bind the people building the systems, too.

Consider two employees answering the same customer complaint with the same AI assistant. It produces a convincing reply based on an outdated refund policy. One employee sends it. The other checks the current policy, catches the problem, and refers the exception to someone authorized to decide.

Same tool, different judgment. An assessment of readiness should be able to tell those employees apart. It should also include routine cases they can handle themselves; referring every request to a manager isn't evidence of competence.

The driving analogy helps here. Being allowed to drive a car doesn't mean being qualified to carry a busload of passengers. Likewise, permission to draft a customer reply shouldn't automatically include permission to issue a refund or leave an assistant sending messages unattended.

But an experienced driver still has to stop at a crossing. Demonstrating skill doesn't exempt an employee from privacy rules, and customers need somewhere to challenge a decision that went wrong. The organization has to provide those protections.

The analogy has limits. It doesn't prove an AI licensing program would work, or that individual skill can fix a system whose incentives are hidden. The navigation company still owes the driver an explanation of the sponsored detour.

Before expanding someone's authority, ask what they've demonstrated with the actual system and the work they're taking on. Then ask which responsibilities remain with the organization and its suppliers.

A more capable tool can help someone become ready for greater responsibility. Access to it isn't evidence that they're ready yet.
