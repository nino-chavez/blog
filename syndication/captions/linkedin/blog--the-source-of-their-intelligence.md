There's an argument going around that most developers were never really engineering anything. They were sourcing answers from Stack Overflow and Reddit, and all AI did was swap out the supplier.

My first instinct was to defend the craft, which should probably tell me something. I've spent years making architectural calls that took judgment rather than search skills, and I wanted that to count for more than the argument allows.

Then I thought about teams I've worked on where three of five developers couldn't explain why the code they wrote worked. They knew that it worked. They'd tested it, shipped it, and moved on, and the reasoning underneath was borrowed from somewhere nobody could name anymore. Whatever that is, it isn't engineering. It's assembly.

And I've had seasons like that myself — stretches where I was moving fast enough that understanding took a back seat to shipping, and "it works" was the only bar I actually cleared. That's easier to name in retrospect than it was at the time, which is the usual arrangement.

Here's where the argument gets interesting rather than just uncomfortable. The average developer's intelligence was always partly external. Before language models it lived on Stack Overflow, in documentation, and in the one senior engineer everyone pinged. The individual contribution was curation — choosing which external answer to reach for, then fitting it to a context nobody else could see.

If that's true, then AI didn't remove the thinking. It relocated where the thinking has to happen.

Which leaves something the "AI replaces developers" framing handles badly. When the sourcing gets fast enough to be effectively free, what exactly is left to be good at? I have a partial answer and no confidence that it's the whole one.
