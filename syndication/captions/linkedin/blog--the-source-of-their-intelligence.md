There's an argument going around that most developers were never really engineering anything. They were sourcing answers from Stack Overflow and Reddit. AI just swapped the supplier.

My first instinct was to defend the craft. Which should tell me something. I've spent years making architectural calls that took judgment rather than search skills. I wanted that to count for more than the argument allows.

Then I thought about teams where three of five developers couldn't explain why the code they wrote worked. They knew that it worked. They'd tested it, shipped it, moved on. The reasoning underneath was borrowed from somewhere nobody could name anymore.

Whatever that is, it isn't engineering. It's assembly.

I've had seasons like that myself. Stretches where I moved fast enough that understanding took a back seat to shipping, and "it works" was the only bar I cleared. That's easier to name in retrospect than it was at the time.

Here's where the argument gets interesting rather than just uncomfortable. The average developer's intelligence was always partly external. Before language models it lived on Stack Overflow, in documentation, in the one senior engineer everyone pinged. The individual contribution was curation. You chose which external answer to reach for, then fit it to a context nobody else could see.

If that's true, AI didn't remove the thinking. It relocated where the thinking has to happen.

Which leaves something the "AI replaces developers" framing handles badly. When sourcing gets fast enough to be free, what's left to be good at? I have a partial answer. Not a whole one.
