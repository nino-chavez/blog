I typed four words into a prompt and got back something that would have taken me a full day to write from scratch. My first reaction wasn't relief. It was a low-grade vertigo, the sensation of the floor shifting a couple of inches to the left while nobody else in the room noticed. Because if four words can replace a day of work, what exactly was the day of work?

Watch how people talk about prompting and the reaching is impossible to miss. We say prompt engineering. AI whisperer. Orchestrator. Lately the metaphors have gone culinary — we're chefs composing dishes, sommeliers with refined palates, curating ingredients and plating with intention. I've used most of these myself. They feel better than the alternative, which is that I typed a sentence and a machine did the thing.

The metaphors aren't really about accuracy. They're about self-preservation. If prompting is a craft then I'm still a craftsperson, and if it's just typing instructions the question stays open.

Being straight about what it involves: knowing what you want, which is harder than it sounds. Describing it precisely. Recognizing when the output misses. Iterating with enough direction to close the gap. All of that is real and none of it is nothing, but it also isn't making. It's closer to directing, or commissioning, or — the one that actually needles — managing. And I have always been slightly suspicious of people whose primary skill is judgment without production.

The crisis lands hardest in the middle. Beginners don't have one, because the tool is simply powerful and they're learning. Experts have a manageable version, because the gap between what the machine produces and what they'd accept is obvious enough to be reassuring. In the middle, where most of us live, the output is almost what you'd have made — close enough that the delta feels trivial, and an identity built on a trivial delta starts to wobble.

Typesetters became designers. Webmasters became developers. Every one of those titles felt insufficient at first, and we're in that gap now.

The people least rattled by any of this seem to be the ones with the strongest sense of what good looks like, which only moves the question rather than answering it. What is taste, and what happens if we stop building it?
