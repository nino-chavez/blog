Six hundred and fourteen agent transcripts were sitting unread on this machine. That's the normal state of affairs, not a confession of anything unusual. A correction you gave. A prompt that finally worked. An irritation you meant to do something about. All of it lives in the transcript. Then the transcript closes. The next session starts from nothing. It re-derives what you already learned.

Mined instead, the pile came back as 1,419 signals across 74 projects. Three kinds of thing fell out of it.

Reusable prompts. The least interesting part, and the fastest to pay off.

Corrections, which are the richest vein by a distance. A "no, do it this way" from months ago becomes a standing prior. The next session reads it before it starts guessing. One of mine was that I want interactive HTML demos rather than video. That preference is why a set of decks got built the way they did. I never had to say it again.

And waste, converted into a number. "The agent keeps leaving stray servers running" is a shrug you make and then forget. Counted across the corpus it was more than a hundred. Killed by hand, one at a time. That was enough to justify writing the wrapper that ended it. The shrug never was.

One discipline holds the rest together: re-derive the number when you use it. A counted figure in an old note is a stale snapshot. Quoting it is how a useful record becomes the next thing that lies to you.

The ledger has to grade what got ripped out, too. The mining tool's own honest entry is that it was rebuilt several times before it held. That entry is worth more than any success it logged. The measure is survival. What's still in the codebase months later, not what got committed.

A prior earns its place by surviving, not by being said. The stack has 1,419 signals and no survival column.
