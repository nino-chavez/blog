The standard story about agentic automation says people move from executors to shepherds. You stop doing the work and start overseeing the agents that do it. Review their output. Approve their decisions. Catch their mistakes.

That sounds like a promotion. In practice it might be the hardest job in the building.

When an agent makes three hundred decisions in the time it takes a person to make one, oversight stops being a safety net and turns into a performance bottleneck. Machine-speed output cannot be meaningfully reviewed at human speed, so review becomes rubber-stamping, sampling, or trusting the system — which is the exact thing it was supposed to prevent.

The damage isn't a catastrophic error either. It's a subtle one, approved, compounding for hours before anyone notices.

Guardrails don't fix this. You can always build them. The question is whether human auditors can keep pace with the systems those guardrails constrain, and faster agents make that worse in both directions at once: more to review, less capacity to review it.

That is one of five objections this piece runs at its own argument. The others are token economics, protocol security, context reliability, and the cost of going greenfield in a world that is almost entirely brownfield. Every one of them lands on both sides of the industry's current split.

Which is what the piece is actually about — not which path wins, but whether walking both is what keeps either from maturing.
