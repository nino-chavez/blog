Five reviewers audited my app and none of them opened a screenshot of it. Eleven were in the repository, taken on a real phone. The report they produced was careful, every claim tagged with its evidence class, and it answered a question I had not asked.

The rule that came out of it is short. If the question is about what a person sees, whoever forms the verdict looks first, before sending anyone else to look. And before reviewers are briefed, the briefs get counted against the ask. Four briefs about process against a question that was three-quarters about the user is visible in thirty seconds. After dispatch it costs a correction.

## Three of the four questions were about her

Minder is a day planner I have been building for one person. Zoey runs a household calendar she does not own and cannot edit. My message asking for the audit put four questions together. How far has the app drifted from its mission? Is every interaction right for what a user like Zoey needs to do, see, and know? What does she do often, and is that quick? What is repeatable, automatable, saved and reusable?

The first is a process question. The other three are about her. The word "right" is asking for a judgment, not an inventory.

The session dispatched five reviewers. One mapped feature directories to the decision records that authorized them. One re-derived the design contract against source. One inventoried reuse and automation. One read the screenshots against the design contract. The fifth was named for the user, and its brief asked for tap counts and request traces. None of the five was holding my question.

## The report was right and beside the point

Every finding survived. The only recorded verdict from Zoey is a rejection, from the first build, on 2026-08-23. Build 12 reports zero installs. One decision record, still marked Proposed, says not to enable a feature in production and declines to name a number for it. Twenty-seven hours later a commit named the number and turned it on. Five feature flags are enabled in the release build while three documents say all of them default off. Several of those block a release.

I read the report and sent back one sentence: "this report feels like it just audited the mechanical parts and none of the application functional parts for a user."

That sentence sent the session to the captures. It opened them itself and wrote down what it saw. The frames are in the repository, and anyone can check the reading.

Capture 02 is an upcoming gymnastics class. The card opens with a heading, "Destination needed for directions." Under it, three lines of policy about why the app wants a map location, then a large filled button to go choose one. Below all of that, in the smallest type on the card, is the child's name. Maya is the weakest element on her own card.

Capture 04 is a dense day, which is the day the product exists for. Between the thing happening now and the thing happening next sit two creation buttons, a date strip, a section heading, and a filter control. The next commitment starts at the bottom edge with its time cut mid-line. The design contract requires "what is now, what is next" inside five seconds.

Capture 06 shows a calendar that has silently gone missing. The notice sits below the fold, its action set as plain bold text. Above it is a half-screen card whose dominant button is, again, "Choose map place."

Ten minutes of that produced more functional defects than five reviewers had. None of it needed a reviewer. It needed someone holding the question to look.

## A reviewer sees what its brief asks about

The reviewer that read the captures did its job. Its brief said to judge the screens against the design contract, and it did. It was not carrying "is this what Zoey needs to see." So it had no reason to report that a child's name had lost to a map prompt.

Delegating a search works. An agent sweeps a hundred files and the conclusion comes back intact. Delegating the looking does not, because looking is only worth something when the one doing it holds the question. Hand it to a narrower question and you get a narrower answer, delivered with full confidence.

The briefs had been shaped by the repository rather than by the ask. Minder publishes seventeen decision records, a traceability matrix, an evidence ladder, a definition of done, and a log of methodology amendments. That surface is easy to grep, and grep rewards process questions. The functional map was there too. The requirements document names forty-three user jobs, FP-0 through FP-42, each with a status. Two of them, FP-3 "Open the destination" and FP-4 "Set a leave reminder," are the gymnastics card. The session found that list after my sentence, not before.

## The repository had already made the same mistake

A screen review of the current build states, at line 162, that four captures "show 'Mark done' buttons on read-only calendar events." A decision record dated 2026-09-01 removed completion from imported calendar events, and the code landed the same day, before those captures were taken. Capture 01 is the imported calendar event. It has a "Choose map place" button, a "Details" button, and no completion control. The "Mark done" in capture 04 sits on an item whose provenance line reads "In Minder · My day · editable."

The review agreed with the design document, which still described the pre-fix defect as verified in source. Document and review confirm each other. Neither matches the frame. A reviewer can have a screenshot open and still be reading a stale document.

It happened once more while the retrospective was being written, and that time the check ran correctly and still missed. One session corrected another on how many testers had installed build 8. The second session refused to take the claim on trust, pulled a traceability row dated 2026-08-28, and rejected half the correction. The TestFlight receipt from 2026-09-01 is the primary artifact, three days newer, and it supported the correction. Pulling a source is necessary. It is not sufficient. A dated prose row is a claim about a receipt, not the receipt.

## Where the story is weaker than it sounds

The handoff closes on a line it wanted me to use:

> Minder's team wrote 96,000 lines of Swift, 37,900 of them tests, built an evidence ladder stricter than most professional teams keep — and never put the product in front of the one person it was built for.

The counts hold. A recount gives 95,787 lines of Swift, 37,869 of them in test targets. The last clause does not. A signed debug build went onto Zoey's phone on 2026-08-25, and the receipt shows her on build 8. What is true is narrower. Her only recorded verdict is the build-1 rejection, and every evidence rung added since measures what the team could verify without her. Shipping became the loop. A verdict was never the gate.

The second weakness is unmeasured. The session's second wave changed two things at once. The briefs were reorganized around Zoey's jobs, and every reviewer was told to look at the captures before reading source. The findings improved. Which change did it, nobody can say from this session.

## What changed

The handoff proposed six rules. They collapse to two, and both are in my working rules now.

The first extends a rule I already had. A verdict formed from another agent's summary is not a verdict until you pull a source yourself. The session honored that for every textual claim and lost the point, because it pulled sources and delegated the looking. The new sentence says that when the claim is about what a person sees, the source-pull is opening the frame.

The second goes into the dispatch checklist. Decompose the ask by kind. List the repository's user jobs separately from its decision records. Compare the brief mix with the ask before anything goes out.

The other four candidates were these two seen from other angles, and I declined them. The next audit of Minder starts with capture 04.
