A friend described the AI he wants as plus ten to a stat in Baldur's Gate. I liked it and pushed it further — a cloak of intelligence. It makes you better. So if your numbers start low, don't expect much magic.

Then I looked up what the item actually does.

> Set the wearer's Intelligence score to 17. The enchantment has no effect if their Intelligence score is higher without it.

It doesn't add. It sets.

We had it backwards. An item that sets a score is worth the most to the character with the worst one. It is worth nothing to the character already above it.

That reversal is the small version of a bigger one. The research everyone cites for *AI levels the field* measures one person doing one job. Count the jobs instead and the answer changes: what the tool is worth to you tracks how many kinds of work you touch, not how good you are at any single one. So the useful question isn't how skilled you are. It's how long your list is.

I counted mine. One category turned out to be work nobody gets hired to do — and I got its size wrong twice before the number held still.

---

## The Research Says the Item, Not the Analogy

Two studies, and they read like the item text.

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond gave a generative assistant to 5,179 customer support agents. They measured issues resolved per hour. Fourteen percent better on average. **Thirty-four percent for novice and low-skilled workers. Minimal for the experienced and highly skilled.**

METR ran a randomized trial with 16 open-source developers. They worked on repositories they'd contributed to for years. Those developers were **19% slower** with AI.

The part that sticks: they predicted 24% faster going in. Afterward, having been measured, they still believed it had sped them up by 20%.

So above the threshold the item isn't neutral. It costs you, and it doesn't tell you.

---

## Then I Should Be Getting Nothing

Except I'm not, and that's the problem with the tidy version.

I'm not a novice. The gains aren't small. So which is it — am I the exception, or is the finding narrower than its headline?

I went looking.

---

## Both Studies Hold the Work Fixed

Read the setups next to each other and the same shape appears twice.

The support agents were doing support tickets. The developers were fixing issues in codebases they'd worked in for years. They were recruited for depth in the exact thing being measured.

One kind of work each. Held constant. The tool varies.

Under that design an expert has nowhere to go. The only surface being measured is the one they're already best at. Neither study is wrong. Both are answering "does this help you at your job," and job means one job.

<Callout type="insight">
A fixed-task design can't detect a gain that arrives as a new task. That isn't a flaw. It's a scope, and the headline drops it.
</Callout>

## Counting My Own Work Took Two Tries

So I counted. What would that even take?

Every repository I have. 104 on GitHub — 47 public, 57 private — plus local checkouts never pushed. 117 in all. 11,801 commits, sorted by the words in each message and the files it changed.

Coding shows up in 7,839 commits across 107 repositories. It's the largest single thing, which is what you'd expect from the one kind of work I trained in.

Design shows up in 88 repositories. Operations in 89. Research in 81. Sales in 52.

Those are numbers, and numbers aren't pictures. So: a therapy practice's rebrand, teal and plum pulled out of the butterfly in their own logo. Nine contrast failures cleared on an athletic-training site without touching the brand color. A social kit — bugs, badges, overlays, share cards, a scoreboard component, reel intro and outro. A tournament series' payout copy reframed as a condition instead of a count, and its app icon cut from the logo. Porting a client's own answers into his site's content, one section at a time.

Every one of those went to someone who wasn't me. None of them are coding.

Coding is in two-thirds of my commits, and about a third of the work inside them. A commit is almost never only code.

Then the part I got wrong. I had built the classifier from a list of work types I wrote myself. It found the things on my list, ranked them, and reported back. Of course it did.

An open pass fixes that. No list. Just what's in the files. It turned up work neither the list nor I had named. Methodology. Prototyping. Handoff notes. Translation. Evaluation. Eight hundred and forty-five commits still match nothing at all.

<PullQuote>
I gave the instrument my own vocabulary and it handed my vocabulary back, sorted.
</PullQuote>

## The First Version of This Section Was Wrong

The open pass told me markdown was the most common file type in the corpus, ahead of TypeScript. I wrote that down. It made a good headline: *I write more than I code.*

Then I went to red-team my own post and the number fell apart.

My collection script stored only the first forty changed files per commit. Large commits — scaffolds, dependency sweeps, initial imports — are code-heavy, so the truncation quietly deleted more code than prose from the sample. The analysis script inherited the same cap. Both numbers were measuring my own bug.

Uncapped, code wins, and it isn't close either:

| | files | edits | edits per file |
|---|---|---|---|
| markdown | 7,404 | 25,424 | 3.4 |
| TypeScript | 13,478 | 34,007 | 2.5 |

I'd also called those figures "files" when they were edit counts. A document revised twenty times counted twenty times.

So the headline is dead. What replaced it is smaller and, I think, more interesting.

**Prose files get worked harder than code files.** 3.4 revisions each against 2.5. Narrow the frame to the two folders that matter and the gap widens: files under `docs/` are edited 6.4 times each, files under `src/` 3.9.

There are 2,766 distinct documents under `docs/` in my repositories. Fewer documents than code files. Each one returned to more often.

And before you assume that's just prompt scaffolding for the agents — I checked that too, because it's the obvious objection. Across all 7,404 markdown files, the CLAUDE.md files, skill definitions, and agent commands come to 478. Six and a half percent. It's specs, methodology, and decision records.

So who normally does that job?

Nobody, and on purpose. David Nunez was Uber's first documentation hire and Stripe's first Head of Docs Content, which makes him about as pro-documentation as a person gets. His advice:

> I wouldn't recommend early-stage startups just go out and hire a full-time docs writer.

What he recommends instead is spreading it: *"I put one line about documentation on each level of the job ladder."* Code and READMEs for a new grad. Systems and end-to-end guides for a senior. Documentation practice for a lead.

So the framing I'd been building collapses in a useful direction.

This isn't a seat AI let me fill instead of hiring. **There was never a person in that seat.** The work exists as a line on somebody else's job description, and it belongs to whoever is already busy.

Which means there's no rate it was previously produced at. No worker whose throughput you could compare. Any measure shaped like output-per-person has nowhere to put this and will read it as zero — not because the sample was small, but because the ruler has no such marking.

That claim doesn't need the number I lost. It needs 2,766 documents that exist and a role that doesn't.

The full argument, with both studies' methods and the model's falsifiers, is in the companion paper: [The Sum of Your Gaps](/blog/whitepapers/the-sum-of-your-gaps).

## What the Count Can't Carry

The census has holes, and the largest one already fired in this post: a forty-file cap that produced a headline flattering to my own argument, and survived until I tried to attack it. It's worth noticing which direction the error ran. The bug didn't make my thesis look worse.

Four more I'd rather name than have found.

Sorting by keyword is crude and the categories overlap. A commit adding a payment flow lands under sales because it touches pricing files. That counts material touched, not anything delivered. Video and photography barely register — 187 and 646 commits — because that output usually isn't kept in version control. And most of the repositories are private, so a reader is taking my word for the bulk of it.

The fifth hole is worse and it's about me. This argument changed shape four times in a single working session. Floor, then breadth, then seats, then this. Every turn came from being pushed, not from evidence forcing the move. A thesis that reshapes itself each time it's challenged is fitting, not converging. How would I tell those apart from the inside?

Two things survive that anyway, because they're measurements rather than analogies. Both studies fix the work type by design — anyone can read the methods sections and check. And 2,766 documents exist under `docs/` in my repositories against a role that nobody staffs, however you carve up the rest.

---

## Nobody Reviews the Biggest Thing

The absence I keep circling is quality, not quantity.

Two thousand seven hundred documents and no one whose job is to judge them. No editor. No standard. No review gate.

The seat that was never staffed never came with a reader either. I can't tell you whether any of it is good. Only that it exists, and that four years ago it wouldn't have.

So if you want the number that matters for you, it isn't your skill in the thing you trained for. Write down the kinds of work you'd have paid someone else to do. That list is the whole estimate.

The cloak works. It just doesn't come with a mirror.
