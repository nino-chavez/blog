The latest girls-tryout campaign for 630 Volleyball ended with two finished Reels, selected by the club director and scheduled on Facebook and Instagram. I did not assemble either one in a video editor.

Over the last few months, our work has moved from editing photographs and footage by hand, to introducing a renderer agents could operate, to having agents produce the video from editable source. The human work still included choosing the direction and deciding whether the result was good.

The useful outcome is a creative production process I can ask agents to run again. The next revision can be made in the source and rendered, rather than reconstructed through a series of gestures on a timeline.

---

## The earlier work still needed my hands

Lightroom and CapCut did different jobs. Lightroom handled photographs. CapCut was where I had manually edited video footage. Removing the video-editing step does not mean the photographs stopped needing a photographer.

There is a specific record of that earlier arrangement. In August, when an agent questioned the footage, I explained that it had come out of CapCut through my manual editing. I also asked us to codify a repeatable production process. That request came after the footage already existed; an agent working on the export was not the same as an agent making the edit.

This distinction matters when describing the progress. An automated export can sit at the end of a mostly manual job. It changes the last step without changing who assembles the piece.

My separate [Lightroom editing experiment](https://ninochavez.co/blog/trying-to-teach-an-ai-how-i-edit-photos) did not demonstrate that learned edits saved me time finishing a gallery. That remains its own result. The club-video work provides evidence about assembling and rendering a video, not about replacing photography or solving every part of post-production.

---

## The composition became something an agent could change

[HyperFrames](https://hyperframes.heygen.com/developers/index) gave us a different production surface. Its video composition is written in HTML, with timing, media, text and animation in source files. A render command produces the MP4.

The important part was not replacing one application name with another. The creative decisions could now be represented in files agents could read and revise.

An image could be moved by changing its placement. A scene could be retimed in the composition. Text could be corrected without opening a title editor. The renderer could then produce another complete video from those changes.

There is still a rendering engine and a browser involved under the hood. “Without opening an editor” describes my production work, not the absence of software interfaces inside the tool. We also used rendered previews to judge the result. Looking at the output is different from assembling it by hand.

That intermediate step made the later workflow possible. A tool agents can operate is useful. A retained composition that agents can inspect, change and render carries more of the work between attempts.

---

## A real campaign exercised the whole process

The club director wanted something more personal than a flyer for the remaining girls tryouts. She supplied a gallery. The work had a real destination and real information to communicate.

Agents built the composition from the supplied photographs, using the club’s typography, colors and event facts. The initial treatment was rejected. The next comparison contained three complete visual directions: a bold sports campaign, a slower cinematic cut, and a more informal social cut.

The comparison used the same campaign facts and duration. The differences were in composition, pacing and how the photographs were used. That made the alternatives something we could judge, rather than three promises about how a future edit might feel.

The director selected the bold and informal cuts. She requested different photographs for the bold version. Agents replaced seven photographs, retaining the opening athlete.

I then asked for the 630 in the opening shot to be clearer. The athlete was reduced and repositioned, and the number moved upward. The change went into the composition, followed by another render and a matching cover.

Those are ordinary creative revisions. What changed was the means of carrying them out. Neither selecting a photograph nor saying that a wordmark is obscured requires me to open an editing timeline and perform the correction myself.

---

## The finished file was not the only thing we kept

The campaign retains its editable compositions and the assets needed to rebuild them. That includes the selected photographs, fonts and licenses, audio, the original-photo mask, and the final exports.

The production record also contains the encoded review frames and the scheduling evidence. The render log records the final bold composition being compiled, its frames captured, its audio and video assembled, and its output validated. The MP4 is an output of that process, not an unexplained file at the end of the folder.

Retaining source matters more than retaining a successful prompt. A prompt describes what we wanted. The composition records what was actually built.

For this campaign, a revision can therefore start from the chosen result. It does not need to ask another agent to guess how to reproduce the previous version from its appearance. Nor does it depend on my remembering a sequence of manual edits.

This is repeatable production in the practical sense: the inputs, editable work and render path remain available. It is not a claim that every future render will have an identical file hash, that every creative choice can be automated, or that the production process is already a universal club template.

---

## The human decision moved, rather than disappeared

There was substantial human involvement. The director supplied the brief, chose the cuts and requested replacements. I rejected a treatment and directed a specific correction. Permission and approval remained human responsibilities.

What disappeared from this campaign was hands-on video assembly. Agents performed that work in source and produced the rendered files. Review still involved watching and comparing them, and scheduling used Meta’s interface.

The October 6 records establish that the two chosen cuts were finished and scheduled across Facebook and Instagram. They do not establish registrations, audience response or a measured reduction in production time. Those would need different evidence.

The next club brief can begin with the retained composition, its assets and the decisions already made. That is a different starting point from an exported video waiting for me to open CapCut again.
